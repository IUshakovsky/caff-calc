How this calculator works

The figure in the panel above is not the FDA's 400 mg reprinted. It is built in three steps.

1. An age and status baseline. Adults start at 400 mg a day, the level the FDA cites as not generally associated with negative effects. Over-65s start at 300 mg, 13-17s at 100 mg, and under-13s at zero. Tick Pregnant and the baseline drops to 200 mg, the ceiling in ACOG's committee opinion; Breastfeeding sets 300 mg.

2. Your body weight. The tool multiplies your weight by 6 mg/kg for adults, 4.5 mg/kg for over-65s and 2.5 mg/kg for teenagers, then uses that number whenever it comes out below the baseline. A 55 kg adult is capped at 330 mg rather than 400. For teenagers the weight-based figure always wins. For reference, EFSA puts habitual daily intake at 5.7 mg/kg and single doses at 3 mg/kg, so the adult factor used here is marginally the more permissive of the two.

3. Your sensitivity. The five-point sensitivity setting applies a multiplier running from 1.3x on Low down to 0.7x on Extreme. This is the one step with no guideline behind it: it is a self-reported adjustment, and on Low it can return a number above 400 mg. Read anything over 400 mg as a personal estimate, not as permission - the official ceilings do not move with a slider.

The meter then scores your tracked drinks against that limit: green to 50%, amber to 100%, red above it.

What this calculator does not account for

Weight, age and a sensitivity setting are the inputs most people can supply accurately. They are not the only things that decide how caffeine behaves in you.

  • CYP1A2 genetics. Nearly all caffeine is cleared by one liver enzyme, and its activity varies across the population with a coefficient of variation of roughly 40% (clinical toxicology review). Two people of identical weight can clear the same cup at very different rates.
  • Medications. Oral contraceptives, ciprofloxacin, cimetidine and fluvoxamine all inhibit CYP1A2 and slow caffeine clearance (same review). Nothing in the form above knows what you take.
  • Smoking. Smoking induces CYP1A2 and speeds clearance by about 40-65%, with reported half-life falling from around 6 hours to 3.5 (2025 review).
  • Liver disease and anxiety disorders. The first slows clearance; the second changes what a tolerable dose feels like, well below any toxic threshold.
  • Timing. This is a daily total, not a timeline. 400 mg spread over a day and 400 mg in one sitting are not the same exposure, and caffeine's mean half-life of about five hours (NCBI) means an afternoon coffee is still measurably present at bedtime.
