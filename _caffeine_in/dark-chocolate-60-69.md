## The strongest of USDA's dark chocolate bands, per gram

USDA measures chocolate in the 60 to 69% cacao range at 86 mg of caffeine per 100 g, which comes to about 24 mg in a 1 oz piece and 96 mg in a full 112 g bar. That is slightly higher per gram than USDA's figure for [70 to 85% dark chocolate]({{ '/caffeine-in/dark-chocolate-70-85/' | relative_url }}), 80 mg per 100 g, even though the 60-69% band has a lower cacao percentage on the label.

## Percentage isn't a reliable predictor

This is the clearest example on the menu of why a higher percentage on the wrapper doesn't guarantee more caffeine: USDA's own figures put this 60-69% chocolate slightly ahead of the 70-85% band. Both, unsurprisingly, carry much more than [45 to 59% dark chocolate]({{ '/caffeine-in/dark-chocolate-45-59/' | relative_url }}) (43 mg per 100 g) or [milk chocolate]({{ '/caffeine-in/milk-chocolate/' | relative_url }}) (20 mg per 100 g).

Because USDA groups chocolate by cacao percentage band rather than testing every individual bar, a specific product within the 60-69% range could measure a little above or below 86 mg per 100 g; the wrapper's percentage is a reasonable guide, but not a precise one, for caffeine specifically.

## Where it fits in a day

An ounce, 24 mg, is a tiny fraction of the 400 mg daily figure the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) cites for healthy adults; a full bar, 96 mg, is still under a quarter of it. It matters more late at night, since caffeine's mean half-life of about five hours ([NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK223808/)) means an evening square adds a little to whatever's already circulating, and more for children, whose smaller daily allowances make a few squares a bigger share of the day.
