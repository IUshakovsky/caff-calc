## The lightest dark chocolate band

USDA measures chocolate in the 45 to 59% cacao range at 43 mg of caffeine per 100 g, which comes to about 12 mg in a 1 oz piece. That's roughly half of USDA's figure for [60 to 69% dark chocolate]({{ '/caffeine-in/dark-chocolate-60-69/' | relative_url }}) (86 mg per 100 g) and about half of [70 to 85% dark chocolate]({{ '/caffeine-in/dark-chocolate-70-85/' | relative_url }}) (80 mg per 100 g), in line with the general pattern that more cocoa solids means more caffeine.

## Still well above milk chocolate

Even at the low end of the dark chocolate range, this band carries more than double USDA's figure for [milk chocolate]({{ '/caffeine-in/milk-chocolate/' | relative_url }}) (20 mg per 100 g), since it has more cocoa solids and less milk than a standard milk chocolate bar.

## A common starting point for "semisweet"

This band covers a lot of what gets sold as semisweet or bittersweet baking chocolate, alongside eating bars in the same percentage range. Because USDA reports bands rather than individual products, a specific bar's real cacao content and caffeine can sit anywhere within, or close to, this range depending on the manufacturer's exact recipe.

## Where it fits in a day

An ounce, 12 mg, is a small fraction of the 400 mg daily figure the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) cites for healthy adults; even a whole 3.5 oz bar stays under 150 mg. It matters more for children, whose smaller daily allowances make a few squares a bigger share of the day, and the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) notes that chocolate's naturally occurring caffeine doesn't have to appear on the ingredient list the way added caffeine does.
