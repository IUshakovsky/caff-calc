## Close to nothing, but not zero

USDA's survey figure for hot chocolate made with milk is 3 mg of caffeine per 100 g, which comes to about 7 mg in an 8 fl oz cup. That's a fraction of an 8 oz cup of [drip coffee]({{ '/caffeine-in/brewed-coffee/' | relative_url }}) at USDA's reference strength (95 mg), and even less than a cup of [decaf coffee]({{ '/caffeine-in/decaf-coffee/' | relative_url }}), which the FDA puts at 2 to 15 mg. The cocoa in hot chocolate is real cocoa, and cocoa solids carry caffeine, but a mug is mostly milk and sugar with only a spoonful of cocoa doing the work.

A coffee-shop version runs a bit higher because it's made stronger. A Grande [Starbucks Hot Chocolate](https://www.starbucks.com/menu/product/471/hot/nutrition) carries 25 mg, and Starbucks notes that this matches a Grande decaf Pike Place Roast, worth knowing before handing one to a child on the assumption it's caffeine-free.

## Why cocoa strength varies so much

The caffeine comes entirely from how much actual cocoa solids end up in the cup, and that varies a lot by recipe. USDA's figure for [unsweetened cocoa powder](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/169593/nutrients) alone is far higher, 230 mg per 100 g, but a mug of hot chocolate uses only a small fraction of that in cocoa before it's diluted with milk. A cup made rich and dark, or doctored with extra cocoa powder, will carry more than USDA's 7 mg reference figure; a packet mix built more from [milk chocolate]({{ '/caffeine-in/milk-chocolate/' | relative_url }}) than cocoa powder tends to carry less, since milk chocolate itself is comparatively low in caffeine.

## Where it matters

For most adults, 7 mg in a mug is not worth tracking; fifty-plus cups would be needed to reach the 400 mg daily figure the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) cites for healthy adults. It matters more for the people caffeine limits are strictest for: children, and anyone in pregnancy following the 200 mg daily ceiling [ACOG](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2010/08/moderate-caffeine-consumption-during-pregnancy) uses, where a coffee-shop hot chocolate (25 mg) is a more meaningful bite of the day's allowance than a home-made cup. Read the cup, not just the name: "hot chocolate" on a chain menu can be several times the home-made figure.
