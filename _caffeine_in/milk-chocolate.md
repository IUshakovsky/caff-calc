## The lightest common chocolate for caffeine

USDA measures milk chocolate at 20 mg of caffeine per 100 g, which works out to about 6 mg in a 1 oz piece and 9 mg in a standard 1.55 oz bar. That is a small fraction of an 8 oz cup of [drip coffee]({{ '/caffeine-in/brewed-coffee/' | relative_url }}) at USDA's reference strength (95 mg): a full bar carries less caffeine than a tenth of a cup.

Milk chocolate's caffeine comes from the same source as any chocolate, the cocoa solids, but it has much less cocoa per gram than dark chocolate. USDA's figure for [dark chocolate at 70 to 85% cacao]({{ '/caffeine-in/dark-chocolate-70-85/' | relative_url }}) is 80 mg per 100 g, four times milk chocolate's 20 mg, and the pattern holds generally: more cocoa solids and less milk and sugar means more caffeine, ounce for ounce.

## Against hot chocolate and coffee beans

An ounce of milk chocolate (6 mg) carries less caffeine than an 8 oz cup of [hot chocolate made with milk]({{ '/caffeine-in/hot-chocolate/' | relative_url }}) (about 7 mg), even though the candy bar has far more concentrated cocoa than a mug diluted with milk and water. That's a reminder that finished-drink figures and candy figures aren't directly comparable serving for serving.

The other end of the chocolate scale is dramatic: [dark chocolate-covered coffee beans]({{ '/caffeine-in/dark-chocolate-covered-coffee-beans/' | relative_url }}) carry actual coffee inside the chocolate shell, and USDA measures them at 839 mg per 100 g, more than 40 times milk chocolate's density.

## Where it adds up

For most adults, milk chocolate is negligible next to the 400 mg daily figure the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) cites for healthy adults; a whole bag of fun-size bars would be needed to approach it. It matters more for children, whose smaller daily allowances make even a low-caffeine snack a real share of the total, and worth remembering that the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) notes caffeine in a food like chocolate does not have to appear on the ingredient list, since it occurs there naturally rather than being added.
