## About a quarter of a coffee per ounce

USDA's analysis of dark chocolate with 70 to 85% cacao solids finds 80 mg of caffeine per 100 g. A 1 oz (28 g) piece therefore carries about 23 mg, roughly a quarter of an 8 oz cup of [drip coffee]({{ '/caffeine-in/brewed-coffee/' | relative_url }}) at USDA's reference strength (95 mg). A whole 101 g bar carries about 81 mg, about the same as a standard 8.4 oz can of [Red Bull]({{ '/caffeine-in/red-bull/' | relative_url }}) (80 mg).

## Percentage is only a rough guide

More cacao generally means more caffeine in USDA's figures. [Milk chocolate](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/167587/nutrients) comes in at 20 mg per 100 g, [dark chocolate with 45 to 59% cacao](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170271/nutrients) at 43 mg, and 70 to 85% at 80 mg. USDA records no caffeine at all in [white chocolate](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/167571/nutrients).

The gradient is not strict, though. USDA's figure for the [60 to 69% band](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170272/nutrients) is slightly higher than for 70 to 85%, at 86 mg per 100 g, so a bigger number on the wrapper does not guarantee more caffeine. Don't expect the ingredient list to help either: the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) notes that when a food contains an ingredient that naturally has caffeine, such as chocolate, caffeine itself will not appear on the list.

## Where it becomes significant

For most adults, chocolate is a small part of the caffeine day. A whole 70 to 85% bar is about a fifth of the 400 mg the FDA cites for healthy adults, and an ounce is well under a tenth of it.

It matters more in three cases. Chocolate eaten in the evening adds to whatever coffee is still circulating; with caffeine's mean half-life of about five hours ([NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK223808/)), a late-night bar is not neutral. Children, with lower limits, get proportionally more from the same bar. And chocolate-covered coffee beans are a different product altogether: USDA measures the dark-chocolate kind at 839 mg per 100 g, about 336 mg in a 40 g serving of 28 beans, more than a Grande Starbucks [Cold Brew]({{ '/caffeine-in/starbucks-cold-brew/' | relative_url }}) (205 mg).
