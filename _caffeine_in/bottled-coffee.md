## Close to a mug of coffee, in a can

Bottled and canned coffee drinks, the ready-to-drink kind sold cold in convenience stores, average 28 mg of caffeine per 100 g by USDA's figures. That works out to about 83 mg in a small 9.5 fl oz bottle, 95 mg in a standard 11 fl oz can, and 130 mg in a larger 15 fl oz bottle. The 11 oz figure lands almost exactly on an 8 oz cup of home [drip coffee]({{ '/caffeine-in/brewed-coffee/' | relative_url }}) at USDA's reference strength (95 mg), just in a bigger, pre-sweetened, cold package.

## A generic average, not a label

This figure is USDA's broad average across the category, and it covers a wide range of real products, from light coffee-and-milk drinks to concentrated cold brew cans. Some canned coffees, like [Starbucks Doubleshot Energy]({{ '/caffeine-in/starbucks-doubleshot-energy/' | relative_url }}), carry more caffeine per ounce than this average and are labelled as energy drinks rather than coffee. The only way to know a specific can's dose is to check its label; this page is a starting point, not a substitute for it.

Serving temperature and format also vary: some bottled coffees are sweetened milk-and-coffee blends served cold, others are black coffee in a can meant to be drunk warm or chilled, and both fall under this same broad USDA category.

## Where it fits in a day

At 95 mg, a standard can sits well under the 200 mg single dose [EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/4102) assessed as safe for healthy adults, and four cans, 380 mg, would still stay just under the 400 mg daily figure the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) cites for healthy adults. In pregnancy, where [ACOG](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2010/08/moderate-caffeine-consumption-during-pregnancy) uses a 200 mg daily ceiling, two standard cans already use almost the whole allowance.
