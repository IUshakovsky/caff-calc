How the calculator works

After you drink it, caffeine reaches its peak level in the blood within 30 to 120 minutes and is then cleared at a steady proportional rate (clinical toxicology review). Every half-life, half of what is left is gone, so the amount remaining is the dose × 0.5 raised to the hours elapsed divided by the half-life. The calculator treats the drink as absorbed at once, which overstates the level for the first hour or so; after the peak, the curve follows the same decay.

A worked example: 200 mg at 3 pm on a 5-hour half-life. By 11 pm, 8 hours or 1.6 half-lives have passed, and about 66 mg remains. It takes until 1 am, two full half-lives, to reach 50 mg.

The default is 5 hours. The same review puts the average half-life in healthy adults at roughly 3 to 6 hours, and individual measurements from 2.3 to 9.9 hours. The slider spans that individual range; if you know caffeine stays with you for a long time, or wears off fast, move it toward either end.

What changes your half-life

  • Oral contraceptives. Women on low-dose oestrogen pills averaged 7.9 hours against 5.4 in matched controls (Abernethy and Todd, 1985), about one and a half times as long.
  • Ciprofloxacin. The antibiotic raised the half-life from 5.2 to 8.2 hours (Healy et al., 1989). Fluvoxamine and cimetidine also slow clearance (review).
  • Pregnancy. Pregnant women averaged 8.3 hours, ranging from 3 to 16, against 3.4 hours in men and non-pregnant women (Knutti et al., 1981).
  • Smoking. Clearance runs about 40 to 65% faster in smokers, and the half-life falls from 6.0 to 3.5 hours (2025 review).
  • Liver disease reduces clearance too (review), with no single figure to plug in; ask your doctor.

Choosing one of these replaces the slider with the study's average. Individuals vary widely around every one of those averages.

Where the cut-off time comes from

Two findings set it. In a 2013 study by Drake and colleagues, 400 mg taken six hours before bedtime still disrupted sleep, and the authors recommend avoiding substantial caffeine for at least six hours before bed. EFSA adds that a single dose of 100 mg, about 1.4 mg per kg of body weight, may affect sleep in some adults when taken close to bedtime.

The calculator gives the earlier of the two: six hours before bed, or the time your drink needs to fall below that EFSA level, which for a large coffee can be much longer. A drink that starts below the level gets no cut-off, though light sleepers may still want one. It works one drink at a time; to add up a whole day against your limit, use the main caffeine calculator, and see caffeine in drinks for every figure and its source.

Test your own half-life

Knowing your half-life lets you predict when caffeine drops to sleep-safe levels. Use a 3-day self-test when you can control timing:

  1. Day 1: Drink 200 mg caffeine (about 2 cups of coffee) at 8 a.m. Note how alert you feel at 1 p.m., 6 p.m. and bedtime.
  2. Day 2: Same 200 mg at 8 a.m., then add 100 mg at 12 p.m. Track evening alertness and sleep quality.
  3. Day 3: Same 200 mg at 8 a.m., then 100 mg at 2 p.m. Compare sleep disruption.

If the noon dose on Day 2 does not disrupt sleep but the 2 p.m. dose on Day 3 does, your half-life is roughly 5–6 hours. Cut caffeine 2–3 hours before that cutoff for your target bedtime. For a more precise number, use the calculator above with your actual drink, dose and bedtime.
